How events ripple

The forces behind the news

Most world events are not isolated. Some act on each other directly: a fuel shock feeds into protests, protests push up a government's borrowing costs. Others never touch, yet move together because the same force is pushing both. Those forces are the drivers. Learn a driver once and you can read a dozen headlines at a glance.

What a line tells you

“feeds into”
One is part of the reason the other is happening.
“affects”
One changes the other's course or odds, without having started it.
“is a response to”
One is happening because of the other.
“amplifies”
The other was already happening; this one makes it bigger or spreads it.
“shares a driver with”
Neither caused the other: the same underlying force is pushing both.
“competes with”
Both pull on the same limited thing, so a gain for one is a loss for the other.

Solid lines carry an arrow for direction: one way when one event drives the other, both ways when each drives the other. Dotted lines have none, because a shared driver pushes both.

Every line also names how the ripple travels, the channel: fuel prices, bond spreads, voters' mood. A dotted line names the driver behind both events. See all drivers.

  2. Cost-of-living squeeze

    What it is. Prices, rents and mortgage costs are rising faster than most people's pay.

    How it moves events. When wages buy less, people march, strike and vote against whoever is in charge.

    What to watch. Euro-area inflation (3.8% in September) staying above wage growth

  4. Sovereign debt and bond markets

    What it is. Governments borrow by selling bonds, and investors charge more interest to the ones they trust less.

    How it moves events. A wider spread raises a government's interest bill, leaves less money for everything else and can trigger downgrades or central-bank rescues.

    What to watch. French-German 10-year spread above 150 basis points
